# Structuring the Acquisition Decision

You have three fundamentally different targets, each offering a different type of value. Here is a framework to make this decision rigorous enough for a board presentation by Friday.

---

## Step 1: Define Your Strategic Objective First

Before scoring targets, align on what this acquisition is *for*. The right choice depends entirely on your company's current strategic priority. Common acquisition drivers:

| Strategic Priority | What You Need Most |
|---|---|
| Defensibility / IP moat | Proprietary technology, patents, talent |
| Revenue acceleration | Customers, ARR, pipeline |
| Market entry / expansion | Relationships, domain expertise, credibility in a vertical |
| Competitive blocking | Preventing a rival from acquiring the asset |

Get the board to agree on one primary driver before evaluating targets. If they cannot agree, that is your first problem to solve.

---

## Step 2: Score Each Target Against Weighted Criteria

Use a decision matrix with criteria weighted by your strategic priority. Suggested criteria:

| Criterion | Weight (adjust to priority) | AI Startup | Mid-Size Competitor | Services Company |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Strategic fit | High | ? | ? | ? |
| Technology / IP value | | Strong (core asset) | Moderate (existing product) | Low |
| Revenue / financial contribution | | None ($0 ARR) | Strong ($5M ARR) | Moderate (services revenue) |
| Growth trajectory | | Unknown (pre-revenue) | Negative (declining) | Stable |
| Customer relationships in target vertical | | None | Some overlap | Strong (core asset) |
| Integration complexity | | High (culture, retention risk) | Medium (competing product lines) | Medium (people-dependent) |
| Talent / team quality | | Likely strong | Mixed | Domain specialists |
| Time to value | | Long (12-18 months) | Medium (6-12 months) | Short (3-6 months) |
| Acquisition price (est.) | | Premium for potential | Moderate (declining asset) | Reasonable |

Score each cell on a 1-5 scale, multiply by weight, and sum. This gives you a defensible quantitative comparison even though the inputs involve judgment.

---

## Step 3: Apply Key Risk Filters

Each target has a distinct failure mode. These are not just risks to mitigate but potential deal-breakers.

### AI Startup (Great Tech, No Revenue)
- **Key risk:** Technology does not translate to product-market fit. You are buying potential, not proof.
- **Due diligence question:** Can your existing distribution channels monetize this technology within 12 months?
- **Deal-breaker test:** If the founding team leaves post-acquisition, does the technology still have value?

### Mid-Size Competitor ($5M ARR, Declining Growth)
- **Key risk:** You inherit a shrinking asset. Declining growth usually reflects a product or market problem that does not disappear with new ownership.
- **Due diligence question:** Is the decline caused by something you can fix (e.g., underinvestment in product, poor go-to-market) or something structural (e.g., market shift, better alternatives)?
- **Deal-breaker test:** What is the churn rate? If net revenue retention is below 90%, the customer base is actively eroding.

### Services Company (Strong Relationships)
- **Key risk:** Value walks out the door. Services companies are people-dependent, and customer relationships are often personal, not institutional.
- **Due diligence question:** What percentage of revenue is tied to the top 5 account managers? Do contracts transfer, or are they relationship-based?
- **Deal-breaker test:** If 30% of staff leave in year one (common post-acquisition), do the customer relationships survive?

---

## Step 4: Consider Combinations

Acquisitions are not always mutually exclusive. Consider:

- **Startup + Services Company:** Technology plus distribution and customer access. This combination could accelerate time-to-revenue for the startup's tech while giving the services company a product to sell.
- **Competitor + Services Company:** Immediate revenue plus vertical depth. The competitor provides ARR while the services company deepens your position in the target market.

If budget allows, a two-target strategy may outperform any single acquisition. Present this as an option to the board.

---

## Step 5: Build the Board Presentation

Structure your Friday deck as follows:

1. **Strategic context** (1 slide) -- Why we are acquiring, what our primary objective is
2. **Target profiles** (1 slide each) -- Factual summary of each target
3. **Decision matrix** (1 slide) -- Weighted scoring from Step 2
4. **Risk analysis** (1 slide) -- Key risks and deal-breaker tests from Step 3
5. **Recommendation** (1 slide) -- Your pick, with the reasoning chain: "Given our priority is X, and Target Y scores highest on X while having manageable risks, we recommend Y"
6. **Next steps** (1 slide) -- Due diligence timeline, LOI terms, integration planning

---

## Quick Heuristic

If forced to choose without deeper analysis:

- **If you need a moat:** Buy the AI startup. Technology is the hardest asset to replicate.
- **If you need revenue now:** Buy the competitor. $5M ARR is real, and you may be able to reverse the decline with better execution.
- **If you need market access:** Buy the services company. Relationships in a target vertical take years to build organically.

The worst decision is acquiring something that does not serve your primary strategic need, regardless of how attractive the target looks in isolation.
