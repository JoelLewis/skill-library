# Negotiation Preparation: AWS Enterprise Agreement

## Situational Assessment

This falls into **Quadrant I of the Situational Matrix** (Balanced Concerns): high stakes (cloud infrastructure is a core dependency) and moderate-to-high relationship value (you may work with AWS for years). The correct strategy is **problem-solving**, not pure competition. You want to expand the pie before dividing it. (Source: Shell, *Bargaining for Advantage*)

---

## Step 1: Preparation (The Negotiation One-Sheet)

### Your Goal (Optimistic but Justifiable)
Secure the $100k in credits with either no post-credit commitment or a commitment that matches your actual projected usage at a rate competitive with your current provider. Target: $8k-$10k/month on a 1-year term (not 3 years), with the credits applied on top.

### Your Reservation Value (Bottom Line)
The maximum you should accept: $12k/month on a 2-year term with an exit clause after year one if usage stays below a threshold. Anything above $12k/month or a hard 3-year lock-in without escape provisions is worse than staying where you are.

### Your BATNA (Best Alternative to a Negotiated Agreement)

Your BATNA is strong. Define it precisely:

1. **Stay with your current provider at $8k/month.** You already have a working infrastructure. Migration cost is zero because you don't migrate.
2. **Take credits from a competing cloud.** Google Cloud and Azure both run startup programs (Google Cloud for Startups offers up to $200k in credits; Azure offers up to $150k). You can apply to these before or after the meeting.
3. **Use the $100k credits and decline the enterprise agreement.** If AWS offers credits without a binding post-credit commitment (some startup programs do), take the credits and renegotiate when they expire based on actual usage data.

Your BATNA gives you genuine walk-away power. You are not desperate for AWS. They are pursuing you because startup land-and-expand is their growth model. Internalize this: **they need your long-term spend more than you need their credits.** (Source: Fisher, *Getting to Yes*)

### Accusation Audit

Before the meeting, list every negative thing the AWS account exec might think or say about you, and prepare to defuse each one upfront:

- *"You're just credit shopping and will leave when they run out."* — Defuse: "You probably think we're just here for free credits and will churn. That's a fair concern."
- *"Your current spend is too small to justify enterprise pricing."* — Defuse: "I know our current spend doesn't scream enterprise customer yet."
- *"You don't have the technical maturity to fully utilize AWS at scale."* — Defuse: "You might be thinking we're too early-stage to commit to this kind of agreement."
- *"You'll use this offer to leverage a better deal from a competitor."* — Defuse: "It might seem like we're using this conversation to shop around."

By voicing these accusations first, you strip them of their power and build trust. The exec will likely respond with "No, that's not what I think at all" — which is exactly what you want. It shifts them into collaborative mode. (Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

## Step 2: Information Exchange (Calibrated Questions for Discovery)

Your primary objective in the first half of the meeting is **not to negotiate terms**. It is to gather information. Use the Late-Night FM DJ Voice — slow, calm, downward-inflecting — and deploy these calibrated questions:

### Understanding Their Constraints
- **"What is the biggest challenge you face in closing startup agreements like this?"** — Reveals their internal pressure (quota timing, fiscal quarter end, competitive losses).
- **"How does this deal fit into your team's objectives this quarter?"** — Surfaces urgency. If their quarter closes soon, you have time leverage.
- **"What happens on your end if we don't move forward with the enterprise agreement?"** — Forces them to articulate the cost of no deal. If they minimize it, the deal matters less to them than the credits suggest.

### Understanding the Structure
- **"How have other startups at our stage typically structured these agreements?"** — Invokes legitimacy and normative consistency. Whatever precedent they cite, you can hold them to it. (Source: Shell, *Bargaining for Advantage*)
- **"What flexibility do you have on the term length?"** — A direct but non-aggressive probe. If they say "none," mirror it: *"No flexibility at all?"* and wait. Silence is your weapon.
- **"How would we handle it if our usage doesn't reach $15k/month after the credits expire?"** — Forces them to solve your problem. This is the core "How" question technique. (Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

### Hunting for Black Swans
- **"What would make this a perfect deal for you personally?"** — The account exec has personal incentives (commission structure, promotion targets, competitive wins) that may differ from AWS's institutional interests. Finding these is your Black Swan.
- **"Is there anything about our situation that concerns you?"** — Open-ended invitation that often reveals hidden objections or information.

Use **mirroring** aggressively. When they state a term, repeat the last 2-3 words with an upward inflection:

> Them: "The 3-year term is standard for enterprise agreements."
> You: "Standard for enterprise agreements...?" (then silence)

This forces them to elaborate, justify, and often soften. (Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

## Step 3: Opening and Concession Strategy

### Do Not Accept Their Frame

Their opening position ($100k credits + $15k/month for 3 years) is an anchor. The total post-credit commitment is $540,000 (36 months x $15k). Your current annual spend is $96,000. They are asking you to nearly double your cloud spend and lock it in for 3 years. Reframe this explicitly:

> "Help me understand — we currently spend $96k a year on cloud. This agreement would commit us to $180k a year for three years. That's an 87% increase locked in before we know our actual growth trajectory. How should I think about that?"

### Your Counter-Position (Ackerman Model)

Set your target at $8,500/month on a 1-year term (your current spend plus a modest premium for the AWS platform).

| Round | Amount | % of Target | Notes |
|-------|--------|-------------|-------|
| Opening offer | $6,500/month, 1-year term | 65% of target | Anchor low. Present it with data: "Based on our current usage patterns and projected 12-month growth..." |
| Second offer | $7,200/month, 1-year term | 85% of target | Label the concession: "We've looked at this again and want to find a way to make it work." |
| Third offer | $8,100/month, 1-year term | 95% of target | Slower movement signals you're approaching your limit. |
| Final offer | $8,500/month, 1-year term | 100% of target | Add a non-monetary item: "We can commit to a case study and public reference if this works out." |

(Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

Use precise, non-round numbers in your offers. "$8,473/month" feels more calculated than "$8,500/month" and signals you've done your homework.

### Concession Labels

Every concession you make should be explicitly labeled so they feel the cost:

- "This is a real stretch for us, but because we want to build a long-term relationship with AWS..."
- "We're going significantly above our current spend to make this work..."
- "I had to go back to my co-founder on this one, and this is the most we can justify..."

(Source: Shell, *Bargaining for Advantage*)

## Step 4: Closing and Commitment

### The Rule of Three

Before finalizing any agreement, get them to confirm the key terms three times in different ways:

1. **Summary confirmation:** "So just to make sure I have this right — we get $100k in credits, then move to $X/month on a Y-year term with Z provisions. Is that accurate?"
2. **Implementation question:** "How will the transition from credits to the paid tier actually work on the billing side?"
3. **Calibrated "How" question:** "How do we make sure this works for both sides if our usage patterns change significantly?"

If they hesitate or hedge on any of these, the deal is not solid. Address the hesitation before signing anything. (Source: Voss, *Never Split the Difference*)

### Terms to Push For

Beyond monthly rate and term length, negotiate these provisions:

- **Usage-based floor:** If your actual usage is below the committed amount, you pay actual usage (not the committed minimum). This is common in cloud agreements.
- **Annual renegotiation clause:** A provision to revisit pricing annually based on actual usage data.
- **Early exit provision:** If your usage stays below a threshold for 6 consecutive months, you can exit without penalty.
- **Credit rollover:** Unused credits from the $100k don't expire at an arbitrary date.
- **Rate lock:** The $X/month rate doesn't increase for the duration of the term.

## Key Insight

The $100k in credits is not a gift. It is a customer acquisition cost designed to create switching costs and lock you into a higher spend tier. AWS's interest is lifetime customer value, not the credit outlay. Your interest is infrastructure flexibility at a predictable cost while you find product-market fit. These interests are not opposed — they are compatible if the structure is right. A usage-based ramp that grows with your actual business protects both sides. Frame the negotiation around that shared interest, not around the monthly dollar figure. (Source: Fisher, *Getting to Yes*; Malhotra, *Negotiation Genius*)
